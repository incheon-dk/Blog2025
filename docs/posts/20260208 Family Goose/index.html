Goose Family

family
Author

DK

Published

February 8, 2026

In Korea, there is a term for a man who lives alone, away from the family for the sake of their future: 기러기아빠 (Gireogi Appa), or the ‘Goose Father’

These men stay behind or travel far to work, sending their paychecks overseas.

My father was one of them.

My father worked alone in Đà Nẵng,Vietnam, whereas my family joined me and lived in 대구, Korea while I worked at 구미 공단동 (Gumi Industrial Complex)

When he was 36, he moved to Đà Nẵng, Vietnam for four years to build a life for us.

Thirty-two years later, I followed in his footsteps as an expat in Korea—but with one significant difference.

I didn’t have to be a Goose Father. My family was with me in Daegu (대구), while I worked at 구미 공단동 (Gumi Industrial Complex)

My family accepted the fact that my father would work alone in Vietnam to support the family and to break free from the debt burden.

Likewise, I would not have considered working alone in Korea without the family.

Moreover, we decided as a family, it was time to return to Utah, after fulfilling 4 years of service in Korea.

The company offered a lot of incentives to keep me working in Korea. The decision was never based on what made more sense economically, but keeping the family together.

Now that look back, I wondered, why didn’t I consider the option of being a 기러기아빠.

My family accepted my father’s solitary life in Vietnam as a necessity of survival. At thirty-six, his mission was clear: break the cycle of debt and provide a foundation. In that era, the sacrifice of the 기러기아빠 (Gireogi Appa) was the standard currency for a better life. The family stayed behind, and the father moved forward alone.

For me, thirty-six years later, the math had changed. When the opportunity arose to work in the Gumi Industrial Complex, the “Goose Father” arrangement was never even a variable in my equation. I simply would not have gone without my family.

After four years of service in Korea, we reached a crossroads. The company presented a landscape of incentives to keep us there—financial rewards and career security that made “logical” sense on paper. Yet, we decided it was time to return to Utah.

In that moment, we chose the integrity of our dinner table over the growth of our bank account. The decision wasn’t based on what made the most sense economically; it was based on keeping the family unit whole.

Now, as I look back, I find myself standing in the shadow of my father’s memory, wondering: Why didn’t I even consider the option of being a 기러기아빠?

Was it a lack of the same grit my father possessed? Or was it the realization that the “success” he bought with his loneliness was a gift intended to ensure I would never have to make the same choice?

  • What does it mean to keep the family together?
  • What is the role of the father in the family, can it be augmented or replaced?
  • What are the factors that keep the family intact despite long distances?
  • What are the factors that separate the family despite living together in one home?

Conclusion: The Flight of the Goose Standing at the crossroads in Korea, with the company’s incentives on one side and my family’s future in Utah on the other, I realized that I was measuring two different types of currency. My father’s generation traded presence to buy security. My generation was being asked to trade security to maintain presence.

I often wonder why the path of the Gireogi Appa—the Goose Father—never felt like an option for me. Perhaps it is because I am the beneficiary of his loneliness. He flew alone so that I would eventually have the wings to choose differently. His sacrifice broke the “debt burden,” but it also taught me that the role of a father is not a function that can be outsourced to a wire transfer.

A paycheck can provide a roof, but it cannot witness a first step, hear the tone of a difficult day, or offer the silent strength of being “there” across a dinner table. The role of the father as a provider is vital, but his role as a witness is irreplaceable.

However, the geographical distance is only one kind of separation. Looking back, I’ve learned that a family can be miles apart and remain a singular, beating heart if they are bound by a shared mission. Conversely, a family can share the same four walls and be strangers if they allow the distractions of modern life to build invisible borders between them.

My father went to Vietnam to bring us together. I left Korea to keep us together.

Today, as I sit with my family in Utah, I recognize that the “economic engine” is only successful if it eventually drives the boat home. I am not a Goose Father, not because I have more grit than my father, but because he gave me the greatest gift a provider can give: the freedom to decide that, for my family, being “together” is the only incentive that matters.
