The petals endlessly fall, Scattering in the wind, The day we meet is faintly far, With no promise of a time.

The Korean art song (가곡, Gagok) “동심초” (Dongsimcho) has a rich and sorrowful background directly rooted in ancient Chinese poetry, specifically from the Tang Dynasty.

The song’s story is a beautiful example of cultural and literary transmission across East Asia.

1. The Korean Song: “Dongsimcho”

  • Title Meaning: The title, 동심초 (同心草), literally means “Grass of the Same Heart” or “One-Heart Grass.” It refers to a traditional ritual where lovers would knot blades of grass (풀잎 or 동심초) as a physical symbol of their unbreakable emotional bond and a promise to meet again.

  • Composition (1945): The melancholic melody was composed by Kim Seong-tae (김성태) in 1945, shortly after Korea’s liberation from Japanese rule.

  • Korean Lyrics: The lyrics you provided were not the direct Chinese original, but a Korean translation that was set to Kim Seong-tae’s music. The Korean poet Kim Eok (김억), a pioneer of modern Korean poetry, is credited with translating the source material (sometimes the credit is given to Kim An-seo).

2. The Tang Dynasty Source Poem

The genesis of the song’s lyrics lies in the work of one of the most celebrated female poets of the Tang Dynasty (618–907 CE).

  • The Poetess: The poem was written by Xue Tao (薛濤, Seol Do) (c. 768–831 CE). Xue Tao was a highly respected courtesan (gisaeng in Korean) and artist in Chengdu, Sichuan, known for her extraordinary talent in poetry, music, and calligraphy.

  • The Context: As a courtesan, Xue Tao was accustomed to relationships with esteemed government officials and poets (like Yuan Zhen) that were intense but ultimately fleeting. The poem is generally understood to reflect her deep sorrow and yearning for a lover who had gone away, emphasizing the impossibility of a stable, committed union.

  • The Original Imagery: The poem uses the imagery of the “same heart grass” (同心草) to highlight a bitter irony: while lovers are unable to “bind heart with heart” (맘과 맘은 맺지 못하고), the frivolous blades of grass are left to tie the ceremonial knot in vain (한갓되이 풀잎만 맺으라는고). The falling petals and the fleeting clouds further reinforce the theme of time passing and hopes dissolving.

In summary, the Korean song “Dongsimcho” is a poignant musical setting of a lament written by the brilliant and sorrowful Tang Dynasty poetess Xue Tao, whose personal life as a courtesan made true, lasting love a distant, unpromised dream.

The Korean song “동심초” (Dongsimcho) is based on a quatrain (four-line poem) from a larger work by the Tang Dynasty poetess Xue Tao (薛濤).

The Korean lyrics you provided (꽃잎은 하염없이 바람에 지고…) are a very faithful Korean translation/interpretation of the final quatrain of a series of ten poems called Chūn Wàng Cí (春望詞, “Words of Spring Gazing” / “Spring Thoughts”).

The specific lines that form the core of the song are from the series’ conclusion, expressing longing and the failure of commitment.

Here is the most relevant quatrain in the original Classical Chinese (Hanzi), along with its pronunciation and a literal translation:

Chinese (Hanzi) Pinyin (Romanization) Literal English Translation
花落不同悲， Huā luò bù tóng bēi, Flowers fall, yet we do not grieve together,
欲問相思處。 Yù wèn xiāng sī chù. I wish to ask where longing resides.
不結同心人， Bù jié tóng xīn rén, Unable to be bound with the person of one heart,
空結同心草。 Kōng jié tóng xīn cǎo. In vain, I knot the one-heart grass.

Direct Link to the Korean Lyrics

The key lines that inspired the entire song are the last two, which directly mention the “one-heart person” and the “one-heart grass”:

  • 不結同心人 (Bù jié tóng xīn rén): (Unable to be bound with the person of one heart…) This is the emotional core of the lament.

  • 空結同心草 (Kōng jié tóng xīn cǎo): (In vain, I knot the one-heart grass.) This provides the title of the song and the central metaphor.

The opening lines of the Korean song (꽃잎은 하염없이 바람에 지고… - Petals endlessly fall in the wind…) are actually adapted from the entire series’ pervasive imagery of the fleeting, sorrowful nature of spring and the passing of time.
