“孫女 Grandpa (할아버지), do you remember when you started writing regularly?”

Well, I believe it was right around the time the Coronavirus (COVID-19) pandemic was affecting everyone. But more specifically, it was during a time when I was working closely with the young adults in the Wasatch Front area

“孫女 And why did you start then?”

I started noticing something I hadn’t heard before—at least not nearly as frequently.

I kept hearing the younger generation mention two words as if they went together – anxiety and depression.

People were struggling.

But because of the pandemic, our ability to gather in person was suddenly cut off.

We couldn’t meet regularly or sit down and talk like we used to.

So, I decided to use the tools we had.

We had a social media community or a group with about three hundred members, and I realized I could reach them there.

孫女 “What did you write about?”

At first, I had difficulty choosing my topic and was afraid I might run out of things to write about.

Initially, it was a mix of random observations and personal recollections, and I usually added some of my photographs to go with them.

I wrote about the things I had learned over the years; lessons from my mission, wisdom passed down from my parents, and the many things I’ve figured out simply through trial and error.

孫女 “How did people respond?”

I wasn’t sure at first. I didn’t get much feedback in real-time, and sometimes I wondered if the message reached the target audience.

However, after some time had passed, and the COVID-19 restrictions began to be relaxed, people started telling me in person.

They expressed appreciation that someone took the time to send out those messages of hope.

They told me it was meaningful way to let them know someone was thinking of them and it helped them get through some challenging times.

孫女 “You studied engineering in college, Grandpa. How were you able to transition to writing?”

I still don’t fully grasp how it happened.

What helped was that I had no expectations—and neither did my audience. But the help came in an unexpected form.

I relied on three guides; a theoretical physicist, a rabbi, and a University of Chicago Professor.

The physicist was Erwin Schrödinger—the man behind the famous thought experiment about the cat. He once wrote: > ‘The task is, not so much to see what no one has yet seen; but to think what nobody has yet thought, about that which everybody sees.’

That quote opened up my thinking about everyday life. I realized I didn’t need to reach beyond my capacity or travel beyond my everyday surroundings to find something worth writing about.

I just had to look closer at what was already right in front of my eyes.

孫女 “I’m curious about the rabbi. What did he teach you?”

He was Rabbi Joseph B. Soloveitchik.

He spoke about individual dignity in a way that gave me the courage to use my own voice. As quoted by Ari Berman at a BYU Speeches forum, he wrote:

’I may have very few good traits, but one trait which I do possess is my inability to imitate anyone else. I always want to be myself and to display my unique dignity of having been created in the image of God…

That reinforced my perspective and became a personal anchor for my voice.

It reminded me that my writing didn’t need to look like anyone else’s. Its value lay in its singularity–a uniqueness that matched my specific background

孫女 “And the third contribution was from?”

“Larry McEnerney, a professor at the University of Chicago.

He taught me the mechanics of how to make connections with the audience.

As an engineer, I was trained to present facts, rules, and data.

But McEnerney taught me that writing isn’t just about sharing information or revealing what happens to be on my mind; it is about conveying value.

He explained that readers don’t read simply because you have something to say—they read because your words help them make sense of their own world.

He taught me to stop writing merely to be understood, and to start writing to be valuable.

I am still working on this, every time I write.

孫女 “So a physicist gave you a vision, a rabbi gave you a voice. Then a writing professor gave you a framework that you are still working on. But who were you ultimately writing for? Who was your audience?”

My initial audience was those hundreds of young adults in our social media group.

But over time, that group shrank. The digital communication quieted down with COVID-19, eventually, it became an audience of just one person—me.”

孫女 “An audience of one? Is it really worth writing then? What is your reward?”

It is worth far more than the time and effort I put in.

By capturing my fleeting thoughts and writing them down, I didn’t just learn how to identify problems or formulate possible solutions.

I learned something deeper: I learned about the merciful nature of life.

When you write things down and look back at your journey, you begin to see the quiet hand that has guided you through the years.

It reminds me of a beautiful passage recorded some 1,600 years ago in the Book of Mormon:

‘Behold, I would exhort you that when ye shall read these things, if it be wisdom in God that ye should read them, that ye would remember how merciful the Lord hath been unto the children of men, from the creation of Adam even down until the time that ye shall receive these things, and ponder it in your hearts.’

That is the reward.

Writing became my way of remembering, and in remembering, I found the source of peace, a merciful Being, who is same, yesterday, today, and forever.
