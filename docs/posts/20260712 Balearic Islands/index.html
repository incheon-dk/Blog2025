A Sunday assembly has a way of reorienting our perspective on what it means to live a meaningful life. Recently, I sat in the overflow section of our local ward and listened to a farewell address from a young man who had spent the last two years in our congregation.

He was returning to his home in Alabama.

His full journey, however, began four years ago.

As a young man, he had received strong promptings that he should serve a full-time mission.

Yet, he initially resisted that call and made some choices that required time and adjustments.

In addition, due to the realities of his autism and severe anxiety, the traditional avenue of a proselyting mission was ultimately closed to him.

Instead, he was called as a service missionary—a role with a distinct mandate:

“Help others come unto Christ by serving with loving kindness as the Savior would. Minister in His name to the one through the power of the Holy Ghost and live as an example of faith in Jesus Christ, repentance, covenant keeping with God, and enduring to the end.” — Missionary Standards for Disciples of Jesus Christ, Chapter 1

As he stood at the pulpit, he recounted the network of individual members that sustained him along the way.

He spoke warmly of the two host families who opened their homes to him, and the six distinct service areas that filled his daily schedule.

He had filled and cleared trays in the Missionary Training Center cafeteria, helped patrons trace their lineages at the family history center, and shared his talents as a music teacher for local youth.

For our congregation, he had spent the past year serving faithfully as our ward organist.

Listening to his testimony, a question formed in my mind.

In our culture, the spotlight of validation often shines brightest on the traditional proselyting mission.

But in the overall evaluation, including eternity, what is it that actually changes a human life?

Is it the preaching of the message, or is it the quiet, day-in-day-out offering of a consecrated soul?

The answer came into sharper focus later in the day.

A group of us gathered at a park 5,650 feet above sea level, a thousand feet above the valley floor.

The occasion was a homecoming celebration for a friend of nearly thirty-five years and his wife.

They had just returned from thirty-three months of service on a Spanish island in the Mediterranean, roughly 200 kilometers off the mainland of Spain.

Their specific assignment was to minister to young single adults—a calling built on the simple, vital fundamentals of love, mentorship, and hospitality.

They cooked meals, opened their apartment, and provided a safe emotional harbor for young people navigating the complexities of early adulthood.

Originally a two-year assignment, they extended their stay, returning just three months shy of a full three-year term.

When they arrived at the gathering, we noticed something remarkable: they both looked younger and more vibrant than they had when they left in October of 2023.

This was especially remarkable for the husband.

It felt nothing short of a miracle, considering he was diagnosed with Multiple Sclerosis fifteen years ago.

It had taken special medical arrangements and complex adjustments to his treatment regimen just to make their departure possible.

Looking at these two examples of service—the young man with autism playing our chapel organ, and the veteran friend with MS feeding young adults on a Mediterranean island—the underlying truth became undeniable.

Both had bodies and minds that required adjustments to the system, yet both found a way to break through their physical constraints to serve the Master.

The events of the day left a deep impression.

I am grateful to live in a community where people do not only talk of Christ, but actively live out the wisdom taught in the Book of Mormon:

“And behold, I tell you these things that ye may learn wisdom; that ye may learn that when ye are in the service of your fellow beings ye are only in the service of your God.” — Mosiah 2:17

Changing a life, it turns out, requires no specific geographical destination or perfect physical health.

Instead, it requires the willingness to show up, pick up the tools we have been given, and minister to the one.
