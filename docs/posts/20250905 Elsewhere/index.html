Didn’t know how to act.

We were gazing at the coffin.

The person had passed away at 55.

Leaving 3 unmarried children at home.

The husband walked over and pointed at various memorabilia on the table and images of her life displayed on the screen.

Then said, she is not there, (in the coffin), but in these memories.

As we think of a person, their collective recollections, the memories they shared with us are brought forward.

We are then able to keep their memories are with us.

They live on – eternally – in our lives

2 Movies, one released in 2016 and the other in 2017 had significant impact on how we view this life and the relationships we develop.

American stop-motion animated fantasy film

Kubo and the Two Strings is a stop-motion animated fantasy steeped in Japanese folklore and myth. The film follows Kubo, a boy who can animate origami with music from his shamisen (a three-stringed instrument). He lives quietly, caring for his ill mother, until supernatural forces—his mother’s sisters and his grandfather, the Moon King—hunt him down.

To survive, Kubo embarks on a quest to find three magical items that once belonged to his father, a fallen samurai. Along the way, he is guided by Monkey and Beetle, companions who embody loyalty, sacrifice, and forgotten family ties.

The journey is both external and deeply personal: Kubo confronts loss, memory, and the stories that shape identity. Ultimately, instead of defeating his grandfather through violence, Kubo redefines the conflict through compassion and storytelling—transforming vengeance into remembrance and forgiveness.

American animated fantasy comedy-drama film

Coco (2017, Pixar/Disney) is an animated film set in Mexico that explores themes of family, memory, and cultural tradition through the lens of the Día de los Muertos (Day of the Dead).

The story follows Miguel, a 12-year-old boy who dreams of becoming a musician despite his family’s long-standing ban on music. When he secretly borrows a guitar from a shrine to his idol, the late singer Ernesto de la Cruz, he is magically transported to the Land of the Dead.

There, Miguel meets his ancestors and discovers family secrets—most importantly that de la Cruz is not the hero he seemed, and that his true great-great-grandfather is Héctor, a forgotten musician struggling to be remembered.

The film emphasizes that remembering and honoring loved ones keeps them alive in spirit, while being forgotten is a “final death.” By the end, Miguel reconciles his passion for music with his family’s values, restoring harmony between generations.

Perfect — here’s a comparison of Coco and Kubo and the Two Strings, framed for an adult audience (20+):

Themes in Common

  • Family & Legacy: Both films put family at the center. Coco emphasizes honoring ancestors and the bonds across generations, while Kubo shows how the actions and choices of parents shape children, even long after death.
  • Memory & Storytelling: Coco focuses on remembering loved ones to keep them alive in spirit, while Kubo portrays storytelling as a weapon against oblivion, a way to transform grief into meaning.
  • Mortality & Loss: Both tackle death openly, but in different tones. Coco is tender, celebratory, and hopeful. Kubo is darker and melancholic, blending the inevitability of loss with the possibility of emotional survival.

Cultural Lens

  • Coco draws from Mexican traditions like Día de los Muertos, presenting a vibrant, colorful afterlife where joy and remembrance sustain family bonds.
  • Kubo takes inspiration from Japanese folklore and Buddhist ideas, with themes of impermanence, ancestral spirits, and transformation.

Tone & Style

  • Coco is musical, lively, and emotionally warm—its message is broadly accessible and uplifting.
  • Kubo is poetic, visually striking, and more contemplative, often leaning toward the somber beauty of myth.

Takeaway for Adults

Together, these films show two cultural perspectives on the universal human experiences of loss, memory, and family. Coco comforts us with the idea that love transcends death when we remember, while Kubo challenges us to face mortality with courage, compassion, and the transformative power of storytelling.
