The Unstable Approach

turbulence
Author

DK

Published

May 29, 2026

My flight from Redmond (RDM) to Las Vegas (LAS) was delayed by 39 minutes. Initially, I wasn’t too worried about missing my connection; it was a thru-flight, meaning the exact same plane would be taking me the rest of the way to Provo.

The pilot managed to make up 12 minutes en route, covering the 720 miles in a brisk one hour and thirty minutes. Although we taxied to the gate 27 minutes late, an incredibly efficient crew and a plane full of eager passengers anxious to get home pulled off a miracle—we were positioned to leave the Las Vegas gate exactly on time. I quickly messaged home: “We might leave on time. Keep checking that flight tracker.”

But Las Vegas is a massive, sprawling airport. The taxi out to the runway swallowed up 27 minutes—nearly triple the normal ten-minute rollout. That ground delay almost proved to be fatal.

When our Airbus A220 finally lifted off, the captain announced a brief 50-minute flying time. Once I had that essential metric, I tuned out the rest of the standard weather briefing. I had originally been assigned seat 28D, but the flight attendants asked me to slide over to 28C so a family could sit together. Because seat 28A remained empty, I unexpectedly had the entire row to myself. It was a brief luxury; I would gain a row-mate soon enough.

Meanwhile, on the ground at PVU, the atmosphere was rapidly deteriorating. Between 4:56 PM and 5:56 PM, the temperature plummeted from 71°F to 56°F. Simultaneously, the wind weaponized, screaming from a gentle 8 mph west-southwest breeze to a violent 38 mph southwest gale. Back at our original scheduled arrival time of 3:34 PM, the incoming storm was just starting to drop the temperature, and the winds had been a manageable 20 mph. We were flying directly into the teeth of it.

At 5:27 PM MDT, our aircraft made its initial descent, dropping at a steady 653 feet per minute. By 5:49 PM, the altimeter read 5,675 feet. Provo Airport sits at an official elevation of 4,496.9 feet. We were just 1,178 feet from touching down.

Then, the world tilted.

Within a matter of seconds, the engines roared to a deafening whine as the pilots aborted the landing, executing a sudden, aggressive go-around due to unstable approach conditions. For the next thirty-five agonizing minutes, we circled the airport four more times, trapped in a holding pattern, waiting for the storm to subside or pass through the valley.

With each subsequent failed attempt to line up with the runway, the cabin dissolved into panic. Passengers grew intensely agitated; the sound of dry-heaving and vomiting echoed through the rows, and a few terrified people tried to bolt from their seats, desperate to lock themselves inside the bathrooms for the remainder of the flight.

In that shaking, violent cabin, the cold math of my navigation philosophy vanished. For the first time, I became genuinely terrified for my life, gripped by the very real thought that this plane was going to crash attempting to land in the wind-whipped fury of the Wasatch front.

Just before our next descent attempt, the flight attendants urgently asked me to slide all the way over to seat 28A. They needed to quickly seat the woman who had tried to lock herself in the bathroom so she would be secure for the impending landing.

As she buckled in next to me, she was visibly shaken. I tried making some light, normal small talk to divert her attention and offer some semblance of ease, but nothing worked.

The panic in the cabin was infectious.

Because I now had a direct view out the window, my own anxiety increased.

Looking down, the entire Utah Valley was utterly obscured, covered in what looked like thick, roiling smoke.

Below us, the surface of Utah Lake was churning, whipped into violent, white-capped waves by the gale-force winds. The whole situation looked like a ready-made scene from a big-budget disaster movie.

Desperate for a connection to solid ground, I broke FAA regulations, disabled airplane mode, and texted my wife at 6:00 PM.

She had already been waiting anxiously in the cell phone lot for twenty-five minutes.

Stating the obvious, I typed: “We are circling the airport.”

She responded almost instantly, trying to be a calming anchor:

“Yes, it must be due to the rain and the wind. The updated arrival time is 6:15 PM. The wind got calmer down here though.”

I replied with the ultimate understatement: “Bit bumpy up here.”

In reality, my mind was racing through the worst-case scenarios. I kept picturing the plane crashing as it slammed into the runway. I wondered if the captain would finally give up and divert us northward to Salt Lake City, or fly all the way back to Las Vegas.

And then the terrifying, mathematical questions crept in: Does this aircraft have enough fuel to keep doing this? Do I have sufficient willpower to endure it?

I realized then that I was the one who desperately needed a pep talk, not the terrified passenger sitting next to me.

In that moment of shared helplessness, my wife on the ground and I both arrived at the same logical and spiritual conclusion.

We agreed: “Let us pray together.”

As we prayed, the violent turbulence and deep uncertainty outside our window remained entirely unchanged.

Yet, something profoundly shifted inside the row. A wave of reassurance washed over me, calming my racing heart.

Then, the words we had all been praying for finally cracked over the PA system: “Flight attendants, prepare for landing. We will be on the ground in ten minutes.”

A collective gasp echoed through the rows; some of us genuinely couldn’t believe the words were true.

Against the odds, Breeze Airways Flight MX 653 slammed down onto the tarmac, safely braking to a halt at exactly 6:24:34 PM.

When I finally walked out of the terminal and found my wife, the wave of relief between us was palpable.

As we talked on the drive home, we realized a beautiful, touching truth: both of us had been completely terrified, yet we had both hidden our fear from each other in those text messages, trying desperately to be the strong one for the person on the other end.

Safely back in my own living room, having survived the violent whims of the Wasatch atmosphere, I made a quiet, definitive vow to myself: I am not taking another leisure flight—and non-leisure flights for at least a couple of weeks.
