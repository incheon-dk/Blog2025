The Hong Clan: From the U-Shaped House to the Red Rocks

The New Year celebration of 1999 was held in the ancestral Hong family home, now occupied by the eldest son. Gathered together were four siblings from the first branch of the family and eleven grandchildren—all descendants of 홍한표 and 황영자. It was chaos at its best. The children did their best to replicate the traditional Saebe bows, their eyes lighting up as they received Saebe-don (New Year’s money), while the elders watched the scene with pure delight.

This house held a special significance for me. Nine years earlier, Sister K and I had stayed here as newlyweds. At the time, the home had not yet been modernized; it still retained its traditional U-shape, a design from an era when families and their livestock lived under one roof, with cows occupying the far ends of the structure.

Even then, I was moved by the depth of their welcome. Despite being a new addition to the family, I was treated as an honored guest; they insisted we take the master bedroom while they moved to the guest quarters. For someone like me, who grew up knowing only a few dozen blood relatives, this was overwhelming. While I can only trace my own ancestry back to my maternal great-grandfather—having never met either of my grandfathers—Sister K can trace her lineage back nearly 1,000 years. In that village, practically the whole town was the Hong Clan, and their roots remained firmly planted in the soil of their ancestors.

Our first visit was a “courtesy call” to meet the in-laws. At a gathering of just first cousins, scores of people appeared. In an instant, I felt as though I had inherited a massive, ready-made family.

Eight years later, the roles were reversed. Three members of that group visited us in Utah after touring the U.S. West Coast. They extended their trip to visit their sibling—Sister K’s father—and since there were four of them plus her parents, we decided to rent an RV for a tour of Southern Utah.

It was a remarkable sight: a traveling Korean culinary spot. Whenever we felt the need, we would park the RV amidst the grandeur of Zion and Bryce Canyons, and the desert air would fill with the familiar scents of home. It was a time for them to relax, talk, and simply be together.

Sister K’s father passed away 2 years late, and his older brother, that welcomed me back in 1990, followed in 2025. I will long treasure the association I had with the Hong family. In a world that often feels disconnected, I am deeply grateful to them for accepting me—a man with a shorter family tree—as one of their own.
