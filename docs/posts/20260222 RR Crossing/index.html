I was born in a place called 부평1, in what is now the northern part of Incheon.

Our lives were divided by the railroad tracks: we lived on the south side, and my aunt lived on the north.

In those days, we didn’t have many toys or structured playgrounds. The tracks were our source of fun—and our greatest adversary.

We spent our days playing in the gravel and heat of the line. Our favorite game was a dangerous one: trying to outrun the trains as they approached the crossing.

Looking back, I realize we must have been the bane of the train engineers’ existence. They watched us day after day, waiting for an opportunity to teach us a lesson that the tracks themselves hadn’t yet delivered.

The tracks didn’t just divide the town; they were the barrier between my home and my aunt’s. On one particular day, a slow-moving train and its crew finally laid their trap.

They didn’t just blow the whistle; they stopped. They caught three of us.

We weren’t just troublemakers anymore; we were “the catch” of the day. The engineers didn’t want our apologies; they wanted our mother.

I still remember the scene as they marched us home—the severe scolding my mother received, the embarrassment of a parent being held accountable for the wildness of her children.

The Migration of the Soul and the Soil

Whether it was the train track incident or the arrival of my father’s hard-earned funds from Vietnam that triggered it, we soon moved. We left the iron rails of Sipjeong-dong for a newer part of the city, settling near the main road connecting Seoul and Incheon, right next to 주안국민학교 (Juan Elementary School).

My mother, always looking at the horizon, began studying the city’s expansion plans. She purchased land near the Juan train station long before the developers arrived. Because she bought ahead of the curve, she secured a future for us at a price that would have seen us thrive whether we stayed in Korea or immigrated to the U.S.

I look at the history of my relatives and see the same pattern. Many did exceptionally well in Gangnam, a place that was nothing but farmland before the upstream dams and the bridges connected the north and south of Seoul. They caught the wave of Korea’s explosive growth.

Now, whenever I visit Korea and look up these relatives, the “what-if” inevitable creeps in.

What if we had stayed? Economically, we might have been just as well off—perhaps even more so—than we are now in Utah. We could have ridden the Gangnam wave alongside the rest of the family.

But then I remember the trade-off.

Wealth in Korea often came with a specific cultural tax. There is no guarantee that staying would have kept our family intact. We might have easily succumbed to the Gireogi Appa (Goose Father) model, trading our daily presence for even greater status or academic pedigree. Most importantly to me, there is no guarantee we would have stayed in the faith our parents chose for us.

In the end, my mother didn’t just buy land; she bought us a choice. She built a foundation in the soil of Juan so that we could eventually plant our roots in the faith and family unity of a new world.

Conclusion: The Best Trade-Off

As I walk through the properties I manage today in Utah County, or listen to the hum of the kitchen in our family’s restaurant, I often think back to that day at the railroad crossing in Sipjeong-dong.

The train engineers thought they were teaching a lesson about safety, but the real lesson was in the scolding my mother received. It was the first time I realized that my actions had a weight that fell on my parents’ shoulders. It was the beginning of my understanding that a family is a single, interconnected unit—when one of us races a train, we all feel the wind of the locomotive.

My mother’s strategic land purchases and my father’s lonely years in Vietnam were not just about “moving up” the economic ladder. They were about buying the freedom to make a different choice. Because they built that foundation, I was able to stand in Gumi thirty years later and choose my children over a contract extension. I was able to choose “the holding” over the “Goose Father” life.

Thomas Sowell was right: there are no perfect solutions. There are only trade-offs.

We traded the potential wealth of a Gangnam real estate boom for the quiet stability of a life in the faith. We traded the status of a high-flying expat career for the messy, beautiful reality of being present for every piano recital and every family prayer.

Science tells us that a child needs to be held to thrive. My parents’ sacrifice held us from a distance so that I could hold my children up close. That is the ultimate “Level-Up.” It is the realization that the most valuable property I will ever own isn’t recorded in a county deed—it is the secure attachment of my four children and the legacy of a family that stayed together when the world tried to pull us apart.
