Refined Narrative: The Korean Mission and the Cultural Divide

My mother’s kindness made our family one of the beneficiaries of the services provided by early missionaries in the Land of the Morning Calm. I remember Elder R serving in Seoul (서울), Daejeon (대전), and Incheon (인천) between 1976 and 1978. He was one of an estimated 1,650 to 3,000 missionaries who served during the first three decades of the Church’s efforts there.

These young men—mostly from the United States, predominantly Idaho or Utah, with an occasional missionary from places like New Mexico or Australia—faced real dangers, including the ever-present threat of 연탄가스중독 (carbon monoxide poisoning) from the coal briquettes used for heating.

They often told us that while the Church might seem like a foreign, “wilderness” faith outside the Korean mainstream, it was considered entirely mainstream back home in the States.

That assertion felt confusing when we moved to the Eastern U.S., just three years after our baptism. In Maryland, we were once again members of a church that felt distinctly outside the mainstream. It wasn’t until I relocated to Provo, Utah, fifteen years later, that the young missionaries’ words finally clicked into place.

In Utah, I understood their perspective entirely. The Church was not just a religion; it was the entire social landscape. The neighborhood was the congregation, and the surrounding culture was the community. Here, I witnessed a unique and sometimes jarring blend of church and state seldom seen elsewhere, realizing that the missionaries’ concept of “mainstream” was entirely defined by their specific cultural enclave.

Challenges of Serving in Korea

The period from 1950 to 1980 was one of immense turmoil and transition in South Korea, following the Korean War (1950–1953). Foreign LDS (The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints) missionaries faced a unique set of physical, cultural, and political challenges that defined their service.1

Here are some of the key difficulties faced by foreign missionaries in Korea during this era:

Physical and Economic Hardship (1950s–1960s)

The immediate post-war environment presented extreme physical challenges, particularly for the first groups of missionaries who arrived in the mid-1950s.

  • Poverty and Reconstruction: South Korea was severely underdeveloped and impoverished.2 Missionaries lived in conditions far removed from Western middle-class standards, often lacking reliable electricity, running water, and modern sanitation.

  • Infrastructure: Travel was difficult and slow due to damaged or rudimentary infrastructure. This complicated the logistics of mission operations, from securing supplies to coordinating with the sparse number of local members across the country.

  • Disease and Health: Exposure to unfamiliar diseases and the generally poor public health environment posed significant risks.

  • Language Barrier (Initial Phase): The first missionaries had to navigate daily life and teach with extremely limited access to resources for learning Korean (Hangul).3 While the LDS Church later developed formalized language training, early efforts were often based on local, on-the-ground learning. The Book of Mormon was not published in Korean until 1967, which was a major limitation in their teaching materials for the first decade.4

Cultural and Social Challenges (1960s–1980s)

Missionaries encountered a society deeply rooted in Confucianism and Buddhism, which made conversion difficult due to fundamental differences in lifestyle and social structure.5

  • Confucianism and Family Structure: The strong emphasis on filial piety and ancestral worship (Jesa) in Korean Confucian culture was the greatest hurdle. Joining the LDS Church often meant rejecting or modifying these deeply held family practices, leading to severe social and familial persecution for new Korean converts. Missionaries struggled to help converts balance their new faith with their respect for parents and ancestors.

  • Competition with Traditional Christianity: Korea already had a strong presence of Protestant denominations (Methodist, Presbyterian) that had been established since the late 19th century. LDS missionaries had to distinguish their message from existing Christian groups, which sometimes viewed the LDS Church with suspicion or criticism.

  • Military Presence and Association: In the earliest years, the Church’s presence was largely built upon the efforts of U.S. Latter-day Saint servicemen stationed in Korea.6 While this provided a foundation, it also initially linked the Church to the American military presence, potentially hindering its acceptance as an independent, universal faith among some segments of the Korean population.

Political Environment and Security

The constant political instability and threat of conflict created a tense atmosphere for foreigners.

  • Post-War Instability: The decade following the armistice was characterized by political volatility, anti-communist sentiment, and occasional civil unrest. While missionaries generally avoided political involvement, the unstable environment posed a constant security concern.

  • Travel Restrictions: During periods of high tension or government limitations, the ability of missionaries to travel freely within the country and secure visas was often challenging. The government had to officially recognize the Church before formal missionary efforts could commence and be sustained.7

An Estimate of Missionaries that served in Korea

While official annual counts of all missionaries who have served in Korea are not publicly released by The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints (LDS), estimates can be derived from the history of mission establishment and general Church growth.

Missionary work in Korea officially began in 1956, and the first Korean Mission was established in 1962.1 The growth in the number of missions—currently three—serves as the primary indicator for estimating the decades of service.

The table below provides a rough estimate of the total number of full-time, English-speaking and Korean-speaking missionaries (both foreign and local) who served for approximately 18 to 24 months during each decade.

  • Rapid Growth Era (1970s–1980s): This period saw the most dramatic increase in Church membership in Korea (from thousands to tens of thousands), correlating with a significant expansion of the missionary force.
