🚗📦 Donating Assets to Start a Private Foundation

1. Steps to Donate Assets

  2. Form the Foundation

    • Incorporate as a nonprofit corporation in your state (usually via Secretary of State).

    • Adopt bylaws and appoint a board of directors.

    • Apply for IRS recognition of tax-exempt status (Form 1023).

  4. Transfer Ownership of Assets

    • Automobiles: Title must be transferred to the foundation’s name at the DMV.

    • Office equipment (computers, furniture, printers): Use a Bill of Sale or Donation Agreement transferring ownership to the foundation.

    • Keep detailed records (serial numbers, VINs, condition reports).

  6. Value the Assets

    • Cars: Use IRS fair market value guides (Kelley Blue Book, Edmunds) to determine donation value.

    • Equipment: Appraisal or fair market estimate (Craigslist/eBay comps, or a professional appraisal if large).

    • Required: IRS Form 8283 (Noncash Charitable Contributions) if the donation is over $500; appraisal required if over $5,000.

  8. Record the Contribution

    • Foundation records the donation as an in-kind contribution at fair market value.

    • Donor receives a donation receipt issued by the foundation.

  10. IRS Filing

    • Donor claims the deduction on Schedule A (Form 1040).

    • Foundation records the contribution on its annual Form 990-PF.

2. Benefits for the Donor

✅ Immediate Charitable Deduction

  • Donor can deduct the fair market value of the assets (subject to IRS limits — usually up to 30% of AGI for private foundation gifts).

✅ Avoid Capital Gains Tax

  • If the asset has appreciated (e.g., office equipment purchased cheaply but still valuable), donor avoids capital gains by donating instead of selling.

✅ Control Over Philanthropy

  • Unlike giving directly to a public charity, donating to your own foundation allows you (and family/board) to decide how funds and assets are used.

✅ Legacy Building

  • The foundation becomes a permanent vehicle for charitable giving in your family’s or business’s name.

3. Benefits for the Foundation

✅ Seed Assets

  • Automobiles can be used for foundation operations (transporting materials, outreach) or sold to raise cash.

  • Office equipment reduces startup costs for administration.

✅ Tax-Free Growth

  • Once assets are sold, the foundation can reinvest proceeds tax-free (except small excise taxes).

✅ Operational Independence

  • The foundation starts with physical assets and potentially liquid cash from asset sales, giving it capacity to begin grantmaking.

4. Example: Donating Automobiles & Equipment

  • You transfer a $15,000 car and $10,000 worth of office equipment to your new private foundation.

  • You receive a $25,000 charitable deduction on your personal taxes (subject to 30% AGI limit).

  • The foundation can use the car for operations and the office equipment for administration — or sell them to create a $25,000 starting fund for grants.

⚠️ Important Notes

  • The foundation must use or liquidate the assets for charitable purposes only.

  • Donor (or family) can’t use the car/equipment for personal benefit after donation (that’s self-dealing, strictly prohibited).

  • Large noncash donations (> $5,000) usually require a qualified appraisal to substantiate deduction.

Organizational vs Operational Tests

Public Charity vs. Private Foundation

Great question! Here’s a clear breakdown of the difference between a Public Charity and a Private Foundation under IRS §501(c)(3):

1. Common Ground

Both Public Charities and Private Foundations are nonprofit organizations recognized by the IRS under 501(c)(3). That means they are:

  • Tax-exempt on income related to their charitable purpose.

  • Eligible to receive tax-deductible donations.

  • Organized for purposes like religion, education, scientific research, arts, or relief of the poor.

2. Public Charity

Definition:

  • A public charity is a 501(c)(3) that receives broad public support (from many donors, government grants, or program revenue).

Key Features:

  • Funding source: Must get a significant portion of income (at least 1/3 in many cases) from the general public, other charities, or government.

  • Examples: Churches, schools, hospitals, universities, museums, food banks.

  • Control: Usually governed by a diverse board, not a small group or family.

  • IRS Oversight: Generally lighter than private foundations.

  • Donor Tax Deduction: Donors can deduct up to 60% of AGI (adjusted gross income) for cash gifts.

3. Private Foundation

Definition:

  • A private foundation is a 501(c)(3) that usually has a single major funding source (like a family, individual, or corporation).

Key Features:

  • Funding source: Funded primarily by one person, family, or company, not broad public support.

  • Examples: Gates Foundation, Ford Foundation, family foundations.

  • Control: Often run by its founders, family members, or a small group.

  • IRS Oversight: Heavier reporting and operating restrictions.

  • Payout Requirement: Must distribute at least 5% of assets annually for charitable purposes.

  • Excise Tax: Pays a small excise tax on investment income.

  • Donor Tax Deduction: Donors can deduct up to 30% of AGI for cash gifts.

4. Quick Comparison

Feature Public Charity Private Foundation
Funding Source Broad support (many donors, gov grants) Single/few sources (family, corp)
Control Diverse board Small group or family
IRS Oversight Lighter Stricter (excise tax, payout rules)
Donor Deduction Limit (Cash) Up to 60% of AGI Up to 30% of AGI
Annual Payout Requirement None 5% of assets

✅ In plain terms:

  • If your nonprofit will be supported by many donors and open to broad public involvement → it’s a Public Charity.

  • If it’s funded mainly by one person/family/corporation and controlled by them → it’s a Private Foundation.

Perfect — let’s go into how the IRS actually classifies a new 501(c)(3) organization at the start:

Default Rule

  • When you apply for IRS 501(c)(3) recognition (Form 1023), the IRS by default classifies you as a Private Foundation.

  • Why? Because it’s easier to assume one person/family is funding you until you prove otherwise.

How to Qualify as a Public Charity

To be recognized as a Public Charity instead of a Private Foundation, you must meet one of the following tests:

1. Institutional Public Charities (Automatic Public Charity)

Some organizations are always considered public charities, regardless of funding:

  • Churches, synagogues, temples, mosques

  • Schools, colleges, universities

  • Hospitals and medical research organizations

  • Governmental units (when acting for charitable purposes)

2. Public Support Test

For most other nonprofits, the IRS checks whether you have broad public support:

  • One-Third Support Test:

    At least 33⅓% of your total support must come from the public (individuals, government grants, other charities).

  • Facts and Circumstances Test (10% Test):

    If you get at least 10% public support and can show you are actively seeking broad support (fundraising, grants, memberships), you may still qualify as a public charity.

3. Supporting Organizations

Some nonprofits qualify as supporting organizations if they are organized and operated exclusively to support one or more existing public charities (e.g., a “friends of the library” group).

What Happens if You Don’t Qualify?

  • If you can’t show enough public support → the IRS will classify you as a Private Foundation.

  • But: You can later convert to public charity status if your donor base broadens.

Key Takeaway

  • All new 501(c)(3)s start as private foundations by default.

  • To be a public charity, you must either:

    2. Be in an automatically qualified category (church, school, hospital, etc.), OR

    4. Pass the public support test (show enough donors/grants).
